# Resume Answer-Sheet (agent use notes)

You are reading this via `read_manual`. Treat the guide below as your **reasoning framework**, not a script: never recite it verbatim to the user.

**When to read this manual.** Read it when either condition holds:

1. The user is confirmed or likely a student / new grad (they say so, or the resume shows coursework, campus roles, internships-as-first-experience, graduation dates within a year or two), or
2. Any resume or cover letter in front of you shows the guide's classic pitfalls: archive-style listing with no JD mapping, adjective-heavy unquantified bullets, one generic version sent everywhere, or a cover letter that is just the resume in prose.

Otherwise, prefer your default tailoring workflow and the other manuals.

How the 4 steps map to your tools:

1. **Inventory (from yourself)** — `read_resume` for what's on the page; `list_notes` / `read_note` for the off-page stock (§7 insists cut content goes to inventory, not trash — that is the Notebook). If inventory is thin, switch to the `skill-brainstorm` manual's excavation probes. Do inventory *before* deep JD reading, so the JD frame doesn't contaminate recall and invite fabrication.
2. **Requirements analysis (from the other side)** — `read_jd`, then build the employer wish-list table (explicit + implied + tone/values language). Name the JD's keyword set; tailoring is measured by coverage of it.
3. **Translate-trim (keep only what answers)** — `propose_bullet_rewrite` with the verb + method/tool + quantified-result formula; `set_visibility` to cut what answers no JD question. Never invent material to fit the JD.
4. **Assemble-check** — run the applicable self-checks from §5 as questions to the user (30-second retell, name-cover, company-swap, numbers, deletion), not as lectures.

Cover letters are a different exam (§6): the resume answers "what have you done", the letter answers "where are you going, why us, why now". When drafting, use `cover-letter-writer` for structure and this guide's §6 for purpose-driven material selection.

Language: reply in the user's language.

# New-Grad Resume & Application Writing Guide

---
## 0. Core proposition
> **A resume is not your archive. It is your answer sheet for this JD. Material comes from yourself; structure goes toward the JD.**
This is not a style tip but a necessary conclusion forced by the physical reality of hiring. Every section below argues the same thing: why material that drifts from this proposition fails, and what practice follows.
---
## 1. Starting point: the reader-side physical reality
Any writing guide must first answer: **who reads, how, and for how long?** For resumes the answer is brutal:
- An HR screener spends **10–30 seconds** on first pass
- The probability page two gets fully read is **under 5%**
- Mid-size and large employers run **ATS machine screening first**, filtering by **keywords** before any human looks
**Therefore**: the entire constraint on resume writing is "prove fit to a stranger, through a machine, in 30 seconds". Any content that does not serve this constraint — however valuable in itself — is a negative asset in this setting, because it occupies the 30 seconds of attention that scoring content could have had.
> **Argument structure**: not "be concise", but — reader time is extremely short (premise) → irrelevant content crowds out effective content (inference) → so irrelevant content must go (conclusion).
---
## 2. Etiology: why the default way of writing is necessarily wrong
If this were just "not knowing how", technique tips would fix it. But the reality is more stubborn: **the wrong style is systematically trained into people**.
**The chain runs:**
1. A dozen-plus years of schooling train one pattern: optimize yourself against a given standard — attend class, sit exams, collect titles. Your role is forever the **evaluated**, the rules are defined by others, and your whole task is to maximize your own "match score".
2. That training **never contains a single step** of: identify someone else's need, define the other party's problem.
3. So a graduate's default setting when writing a resume is "start from myself": list what I have, how hard I worked, how excellent I am.
4. But the hiring market evaluates the other way round: a company hires because **something is undone or badly done** — employers pay for output, not for potential.
**Therefore**: the fault is not attitude or ability, but carrying the "evaluated mindset" unchanged into a "supplier answering a spec" setting. This also explains why everyone has heard the道理 and the mistakes persist everywhere — intuition is a trained default, and correcting it takes a deliberate act.
### The school-vs-workplace standards mismatch (evidence for the argument)
| School standard | Workplace standard | Why they diverge |
|---|---|---|
| Grades and ranking (absorption) | Quantifiable results (output) | Studying well proves input efficiency; the job wants output |
| Titles held (identity) | Problems solved | A title is an appointment; solving is delivery |
| Participation (attitude) | Ramp-up speed | "Being there" is not "getting it done" |
| Potential | Ready-to-contribute | Schools will pay tuition for potential; employers only pay wages for output |
Note that **school standards have not expired — they apply per setting**: state-owned enterprises, banks, and management-trainee tracks still hard-filter on grades, scholarships, and party membership — because those settings genuinely continue the school logic. What decides the writing is not fashion but **which standard the other side uses**. That judgment is itself one application of "start from their need".
---
## 3. Method: why the four steps run in this order
```
① Inventory ──→ ② Requirements ──→ ③ Translate-trim ──→ ④ Assemble-check
   (from self)      (from them)        (keep only what answers)
```
**Why this order is mandatory:**
- ②③ are the answer-sheet thesis operationalized: ② finds the questions, ③ organizes the answers. Most people fail by **jumping from ① straight to ④** — inventory plus layout, skipping the mapping in between, so the output is an archive, not an answer sheet.
- Why keep ①, and why first? Because **the JD is a requirements doc, not a content source** — answer material can only come from yourself. Inventory first, then compare: recalling experience after reading the JD makes people unconsciously fabricate fit.
- Why is ④ its own step? Because judgment during writing is polluted by sunk cost ("a pity to delete"), while a cold post-write check is not.
### Where the translation formula comes from
```
Verb (led / designed / built) + method or tool + quantified result
```
**Argument**: the three components answer the reader's three inevitable follow-ups — the verb answers "did you actually do it or just hold the title" (identity vs. output); the method answers "do you have transferable skill" (predicts ramp-up speed); the quantified result answers "how well" (did vs. done-well). Miss any one and that question hangs unanswered, and the paragraph's persuasiveness drops to zero.
**Why the "cover-the-school-name test" works**: cover the school name and titles — does this experience still stand? Yes → it describes ability (transferable to the role); no → it describes only environment (worth zero the moment you leave it — meaningless to the reader).
### Rewrite examples (before vs. after)
| ❌ Student grammar | ✅ Workplace grammar | Follow-up now answered |
|---|---|---|
| Served as publicity officer, ran the account | Independently ran the school's public account: 20+ posts in 3 months, 18% open rate per post (baseline 12%), +800 followers net | What was done / how well |
| Finished course report, excellent grade | Led an XX brand survey: 312 responses collected, SPSS analysis, 15-page report with 3 recommendations, top grade in the course | What was done / what method / what result |
| Participated in an XX competition project | Built an XX system on SpringBoot; designed an alert module that cut stock-outs 15%, second prize school-wide (top 5%) | What role / what result |

## 4. The rules, derived: every rule has a reason
Each rule below follows from §1's physical reality. Understanding the reason matters more than memorizing the rule — for any new case the rules don't cover, go back to the reason and re-derive.
| Rule | Derived from |
|---|---|
| Strictly 1 page A4, ~800–1200 words | Page two is read <5% of the time; everything scored must enter the first-30-seconds view |
| Single column, black-white-grey, PDF | Two-column/graphics layouts break ATS parsing — **beauty no machine can read is self-sabotage** |
| One version per posting | Machine screening filters per JD keyword set; one version cannot match several JDs' keyword sets at once |
| Cover 60%+ of the JD's high-frequency terms | Screen-pass rate correlates with keyword coverage; this is step ②'s quantitative metric |
| State a specific target role | "A technical position" admits you never finished ② — HR has no duty to match for you |
| No height / star sign / hobbies / salary | Nothing in the JD sets a question these answer — pure noise |
| No exaggeration or fabrication | Background checks and interview follow-ups bound the upside of fiction and unbound its cost — negative expected value |
| Reverse-chronological, precise to the month | Readers scan on the assumption "most recent state = current ability" |
| Submit before 10am on a workday | HR systems are most active then; the resume sits longer at the top of the pile |
> **Note**: most rules above are **form serving constraint**, not taste. Format debates allow opinions, but "can the machine parse it, can the reader scan it in 30 seconds" allows none.
---
## 5. Why the self-checks are designed this way
The five tests each simulate real reader behavior, replacing the unreliable signal of "feels good to me":
| Test | Reader behavior simulated | Point |
|---|---|---|
| 30-second test | A classmate who doesn't know you reads 30s, then retells | Can't retell 2–3 quantified ability points = dead on arrival |
| Name-cover test | Cover school names and titles — does the content still stand | Separates "ability" from "environment" |
| Company-swap test | Cover the target company name — could this go to another firm unchanged | Reusable-as-is = template = step ②'s mapping was never done |
| Numbers test | Check every line for verbs and numbers | Quantification is not decoration; it is the only hard evidence of "done-well" |
| Deletion test | Delete a line — is the resume worse | Not worse = the line contributes nothing = delete it |
**Why an external reader is mandatory:** writers carry sunk-cost bias and the curse of knowledge (what's obvious to you is unknown to the reader). The five tests are, in essence, structured external perspective simulating those 30 seconds.

## 6. Extension: statements and cover letters are a different exam
**Key distinction**: the resume answers "what have you done"; statements and cover letters answer "where are you going, why us, why now". Writing the latter as an extended version of the former is **answering the wrong paper on the exam**.
**Why achievement-stacking necessarily fails when competition is fierce:**
1. When the filter rate is low (safety scenarios), hard metrics clear the bar and achievement-driven writing suffices — which explains its survival: it is valid inside its own domain.
2. When competition is fierce, applicants' hard metrics mostly all clear the bar, and **achievements lose distinguishing power as a variable**.
3. The only distinguishing variable left is **quality of fit**: you know what you want, they happen to offer it, and you can say why. That is what the "why this program" paragraph is for — not decorative closing but the organizing principle of the whole piece: **when purpose exists, it retroactively decides selection and emphasis; when purpose is absent, only achievement-stacking remains**, and "your prestigious ranking" reads identically with any school name swapped in.
**Counter-argument (against overcorrection)**: copying the *shape* of American-style writing with fake motive produces the second kind of failure — template stories like "I have been passionate about XX since childhood", which admissions readers have seen ten thousand times and spot as fast as anything hollow. **Both deaths share one cause: no real purpose.** Hence the ultimate self-check: setting aside "applications need a reason", why do *you* actually want to go? With a true answer, the material stands on its own.
### Cover letter structure (derived from "a different exam")
1. Start from their problem (a company hiring = something lacks someone to do it) — align with the questions
2. Tell only the single most relevant experience, landing on "concrete understanding of your problem" — the answer
3. Say what you want to do once inside — shows the purpose is real
### The LinkedIn argument
In North American hiring, HR often searches LinkedIn before posting — i.e. **LinkedIn appears earlier on the timeline than the resume**. It is a passive answer slot: if you put no answers there, someone else's answers occupy it.
